The short answer

Make is where automation stops being a party trick and starts being infrastructure. You draw your process on a canvas, app by app, step by step, and Make runs it: every new order copied to your accounts sheet, every form entry checked against your CRM and pushed to your email tool. The drawing part is the point and the problem. Make gives you routers, filters, iterators, aggregators and proper error handling, the vocabulary of a small programming language, and in exchange it charges a fraction of what the simpler tools charge per action. Ten thousand actions a month cost $12 billed annually. The trade is your time learning the builder. If your processes are simple and few, that trade may not pay. If they are repetitive, multi step and growing, it pays quickly and keeps paying.

What it is

Make is a visual automation platform, known until 2022 as Integromat. The unit of work is a scenario: a chain of modules on a canvas, where each module is one app doing one thing. A trigger module starts the chain, perhaps a new row in a spreadsheet, a submitted form, a webhook arriving from your shop. Action modules follow: search the CRM, create a record, send a message, write a row somewhere else. Between them sit the tools that make Make different from a simple connector: routers that split a flow into branches, filters that let only matching data through, iterators that process a list one item at a time, aggregators that gather results back into one bundle, and error handlers that decide what happens when an app says no.

Every time a module performs an action, it consumes credits. Credits replaced the older unit, operations, on 27 August 2025: most actions cost exactly one credit, while some of Make's own AI features cost more. Routers and error handler modules cost nothing, which matters, because it means branching logic and safety nets are free and only real work is metered. Make connects to more than 1,000 third party apps on its own count, and where no module exists, a custom app can talk to any service with a public API. There are also more than 300 REST API endpoints for managing Make itself.

How the credit meter actually runs

Most automation pricing is vague until the bill arrives. Make's is mechanical, and the mechanics are worth understanding before you build anything, because the same scenario can cost two credits or two hundred depending on how it is drawn.

A credit is spent per module action, not per scenario run. A five step scenario that fires a hundred times a month spends five hundred credits, plus whatever its searches and lookups cost along the way. Polling is the quiet spender: a scenario that checks an app for new data every fifteen minutes spends a credit on every check that runs a search module, whether or not anything new exists, so trigger choice changes your bill. Webhooks, where the app pushes data to Make the moment something happens, are usually the cheaper pattern. Iterators multiply honestly: process a list of fifty rows and the modules after the iterator run fifty times. The editor shows consumption as you build, but the design choices are yours.

The failure mode is equally mechanical. Run out of credits mid month and your scenarios simply stop until credits are added. Incoming webhooks queue up to your queue limit and are processed once the scenarios resume, and polling scenarios pick up from their last successful run, so a short outage is recoverable rather than catastrophic. It is still an outage, and it will happen at the worst moment of the month, because that is when volume peaks. The defences are unglamorous: buy headroom, turn on extra credit auto purchase, and check the usage screen before your busiest week rather than after it.

Where it wins

  • Price per action that undercuts the field by a wide margin. Core at $12 a month billed annually includes 10,000 credits, and raising the volume raises the bill on the same plan, not to a new plan tier with a sales call attached
  • Priced per organisation, not per seat. A team of eight shares one credit pool and one bill, where per seat rivals charge eight times before anyone automates anything
  • Real logic, not just connections. Routers, filters, iterators, aggregators and error handlers are built in, and the plumbing modules cost no credits, so conditional flows are a design choice rather than a paid upgrade
  • Error handling treated as a first class feature. You decide per module whether a failure rolls back, retries, skips or stops the run, which is the difference between automation you trust overnight and automation you babysit
  • Depth of connection. More than 1,000 apps with ready made modules, custom apps for anything with a public API, and an HTTP module for everything else
  • A free plan that teaches the real product. 1,000 credits a month and 2 active scenarios is enough to build and run two genuine automations, not a screenshot tour

Where it frustrates

  • The learning curve is the price of the price. The canvas, the module settings and the data mapping between steps all assume you will invest hours learning them. Simple tools onboard in minutes; Make onboards in evenings
  • Credits are spent by design choices, and beginners design expensively. Polling triggers, unnecessary searches and iterators over long lists can burn a month of credits on a scenario that looks innocent
  • When the credits end, everything stops. There is no soft landing or overage grace: scenarios halt until you add credits, and only queued webhooks survive the gap
  • Free plan limits bite fast. Two active scenarios and a fifteen minute minimum interval between scheduled runs rules out anything time sensitive, and logs are kept for only 7 days, so debugging last week's failure is archaeology
  • Usage allowances are tighter than the credit numbers suggest. Each 10,000 credits brings 5 GB of data transfer and 10 MB of data storage, which is fine for records and messages and limiting for file heavy flows
  • Downgrading is not graceful. Move down a plan and any active scenario using a higher plan feature is stopped, so the plan you build on is partly a commitment

The AI, and its price tag

Make's AI comes in two forms. There are AI modules inside scenarios, which can summarise text, classify incoming messages, extract details from documents and draft replies as steps in a flow, and there are Make's AI agents, which sit above scenarios and work towards a goal across the apps you connect. Both suit a small business: the inbox that sorts itself, the lead enquiry that arrives pre summarised, the ticket categorised before a human sees it.

The billing needs reading carefully. Most module actions cost one credit, but some features that run on Make's own AI provider consume more than one credit per action, so an AI heavy scenario drains the pool faster than the step count suggests. You cannot route around that meter with a general AI subscription you already pay for: a ChatGPT or Claude subscription does not plug into Make's AI provider features. What you can do is connect your own OpenAI account as an ordinary app module in a scenario, paying the standard one credit per module action plus whatever your own API usage costs on that side. For most small businesses that is the sensible pattern anyway: your own key, your own API bill, and Make charging only for the plumbing. Just do not budget an AI scenario at one credit a step until you have checked which steps run on Make's provider.

Watching it work: logs, limits and support

Automation you cannot inspect is automation you will eventually switch off, so the operational layer matters. Every scenario run is logged, and the retention is a plan gate: 7 days on Free, 30 days on Core and above. Pro adds full text search across execution logs, which sounds dull until you are hunting one failed order among ten thousand runs, at which point it is the whole product. Pro also adds priority execution, so your scenarios jump the processing queue at busy times, and custom variables for tidier builds. Teams, as the name says, is about people rather than power: team roles that control who can edit what, and shared scenario templates so a second office or a second client starts from your proven flow instead of a blank canvas.

Support quality is where we score Make a middling 6, and the reasoning is structural. The documentation is extensive and the community is large and technically generous, but a tool this deep generates questions documentation cannot always answer, and a business without a technical member of staff will feel that gap on a bad day. Budget a learning allowance, or a freelancer for the first builds, and the score matters less.

Leaving Make

The exit is better than most, with one hard boundary. A scenario can be exported as a blueprint, a JSON file you can save, share and import into another Make organisation, so your process designs are not hostages. What does not travel is everything around the design: the connections to your apps, which hold your credentials and must be recreated, and the data stores your scenarios have accumulated. Move to a rival and the blueprint is a readable map of what to rebuild, not a migration. Two plan mechanics matter before you ever leave. Downgrade, and scenarios using higher plan features are stopped rather than degraded. Cancel, and the organisation drops to the Free plan at the end of the term, where the 2 scenario and 1,000 credit limits apply to whatever is left running. Export your blueprints while you are still a happy customer; it takes minutes and it is your insurance policy.

If Make is not the one: the alternatives

Three rivals cover the same ground with the trade offs in different places. We have not scored these in full reviews yet, so the verdicts below are one line judgements rather than Sensible Scores.

Zapier. The name most people reach for first, and the easiest automation tool to learn: pick a trigger, pick an action, switch it on. The bill is the argument against it, because Zapier charges per task and the cost per action at volume is several times Make's cost per credit. Pick Zapier if your automations are few and simple. Pick Make the moment the monthly task count starts with a comma.

n8n. The open source option, and the most technical of the three. Self host it and the per action cost falls to your server bill; use its cloud version and it prices between Make and Zapier. The catch is that you are now running infrastructure, or paying someone who does. Pick n8n if you have technical skills in house, or data you would rather keep on your own machines.

Pabbly Connect. The budget challenger, known for lifetime style deals and simple per task pricing that undercuts Zapier heavily. Its app library is smaller than Make's and the builder is plainer. Pick Pabbly if your apps are mainstream, your flows are simple, and a one off deal matters more than depth. Check the app list against your stack before the lifetime price tempts you.

Questions buyers actually ask

What exactly is a credit? One module action. When a scenario reads a record, writes a row, sends a message or transforms data, that step consumes a credit, and most steps consume exactly one. Routers and error handlers consume none, and some of Make's AI provider features consume more than one. A scenario with six steps that runs 500 times in a month costs roughly 3,000 credits, before any AI steps.

What happens when my credits run out? Your scenarios stop. Nothing degrades gracefully and nothing bills you silently in the background: the automation halts until you add credits or the next term begins. Incoming webhooks queue up to your queue limit and are processed when the scenarios resume, and scheduled scenarios catch up from their last successful run. The practical fixes are buying headroom, or switching on extra credit auto purchase on Core, Pro or Teams so a bundle is bought before the pool runs dry.

Make or Zapier at 10,000 actions a month? On Make, 10,000 credits on Core cost $12 a month billed annually, $144 a year. Zapier charges per task, and at 10,000 tasks a month you are on one of its higher tiers at several times Make's per action cost; check its pricing page for the current figure, because its tiers move. At this volume Make is dramatically cheaper and Zapier is dramatically easier. Which wins depends on whether you would rather spend money or evenings.

Is the free plan actually usable? Yes, within narrow walls. You get 1,000 credits a month and 2 active scenarios, with scheduled runs at most every fifteen minutes and logs kept for 7 days. That is enough for two real automations, for example a form to CRM to email chain and a daily orders digest, and it is the right way to learn the builder. It is not enough for anything time sensitive.

Can I export my scenarios if I leave? Yes. Each scenario exports as a blueprint file in JSON, which you can keep and import into another Make organisation. Your app connections and your data stores do not move with it, and no rival platform imports Make blueprints, so treat the export as documentation and insurance rather than a transfer. It is still more than several rivals offer.

Do unused credits roll over? No. Credits expire at the end of the term, monthly or annual depending on how you pay. Buy the volume you will use in a typical month and cover the peaks with extra credit bundles, which can be bought in 1,000s or 10,000s, rather than paying all year for December's volume.

Verdict

Make is the automation tool we would point a growing business at once its processes stop fitting in a simple trigger and action tool, and it earns that on arithmetic as much as features: $12 a month for 10,000 actions, priced per organisation, with logic and error handling the simpler tools do not offer. The conditions are real. Somebody has to learn the builder, the credit meter rewards design and punishes improvisation, and the day the credits run out is the day the automation stops. For a solo operator with two simple flows, Zapier's ease may honestly be worth its price. For an online seller syncing orders, stock and messages across five apps, or an agency running the same proven flows for client after client, Make is the value pick in this category, and it is not particularly close.
