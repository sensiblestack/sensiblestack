Both tools were used first hand, and Zapier is the one used day to day because it was easier.

The short answer

If your automations run in straight lines, a trigger, a step or two, a done, and the apps you need are household names, Zapier. It is the quickest automation tool to learn, its catalogue is the biggest in the business, and its meter only runs when work actually succeeds.

If your automations branch, loop over lists of records, or do several things with each piece of data, Make. The canvas looks more technical because it is, and in exchange it does the complicated shapes Zapier makes you approximate, at a fraction of the price per unit of work.

If you are a one person business choosing today, the honest split is this: Zapier charges for results and forgives idle checking; Make charges for everything, including checking, and is still cheaper for most real workloads. Start on Make Core if you will build anything with a branch in it. Start on Zapier if your first three automations are all straight lines and you want them running this afternoon.

What each one is

Make builds automations as scenarios on a visual canvas: a module for each app, joined up on a map, with routers that split the flow, filters that gate it, and tools that iterate over lists of records and aggregate them back together. It is priced in credits, and the credit is the thing to understand before anything else: almost every module run consumes one, including the run that only checks whether anything new has arrived. That single rule explains both Make's low prices and its one famous trap.

Zapier builds automations as Zaps: a trigger, then a line of steps underneath it. It is priced in tasks, and its rule is the mirror image of Make's: a task is only counted when an action step completes successfully. Checking for new data is free, triggers are free, failed actions are free, and the built in logic tools are free. What you pay for is breadth and ease: more than 9,000 connected apps, and an editor a beginner can use to finish a first Zap before lunch.

Pricing at a real small business size

Checked against Make's and Zapier's pricing pages and help centres, November 2026. Take one realistic business: a solo operator with a lead form that feeds a CRM, a scheduled check on a spreadsheet or inbox, and a couple of notification automations, running a few hundred events a month.

Make. Core, at $12 a month billed annually, includes 10,000 credits a month. The lead form automation, if it starts from a webhook or an instant trigger, costs a handful of credits per lead: one to fetch, one to create the CRM record, one to send the notification. The scheduled check is the line item to watch. Checking every 15 minutes costs 96 credits a day, about 2,880 a month, before a single lead arrives, because Make charges for the check itself. Slow the check to hourly and it costs about 720. Either way it fits inside Core with most of the allowance left over. The same shape of usage on the free plan's 1,000 credits would not fit at all: the checker alone would spend the free plan in about ten days.

Zapier. Professional, from $19.99 a month billed annually, includes 750 tasks a month. The scheduled check costs nothing while it waits, on any plan, which is the genuine kindness in Zapier's model. The bill arrives when things happen: every successful action in every run counts, so a Zap with a trigger and three actions, firing 150 times a month, uses 450 tasks. Add the lead form Zap doing similar work and the entry tier is spent, and the tiers above it are where Zapier's price climbs. Note also that the free plan's two step limit means the lead form automation above, a trigger plus two actions, cannot even be built for free: multi step Zaps are a paid feature.

The shape is the story: Make meters motion, Zapier meters results. Motion is cheaper per unit and easier to waste. Results are dearer per unit and impossible to waste, because a task only exists when the work got done.

Where Make wins

  • Price per unit of work: 10,000 credits for $12 a month billed annually, against 750 tasks for $19.99. The units are not identical, a credit is a module run and a task is a successful action, but the gap is wide enough to survive the difference
  • Branching and data work: routers, iterators and aggregators are native parts of the builder, not steps bolted onto a list
  • Error handling you design: handler modules sit on the canvas where the failure happens, and they cost no credits
  • Interval control: you set how often a scenario checks, down to every minute on paid plans
  • One price shape at every size: plans differ by features and credit tiers, not by a per seat charge

Where Zapier wins

  • The catalogue: 9,000+ apps, so the niche tool in your stack is far more likely to be there already
  • Free checks: polling never touches your task count, so an automation can watch all day and only bill you when something happens
  • Failed work is free: an action that does not complete does not count, so you pay for outcomes, not attempts
  • The fastest start in the category: a two step Zap on the free plan takes minutes, and the editor explains itself as you go
  • Logic that does not inflate the bill: filters, paths and formatter steps are all free, so adding conditions costs nothing

Where each one frustrates

Make: the meter, always. Credits are spent on checks that find nothing, on test runs, and on every module in a long scenario, and unused credits expire at the end of the term rather than rolling over. We have written up the worst version of this in our journal: credits disappearing from a scenario that was only ever checking, politely, every 15 minutes. The other frustration is the first hour. The canvas, the vocabulary of bundles and operations, and the sheer number of settings make Make slower to feel at home in than Zapier.

Zapier: the arithmetic of success. Because every successful action counts, a Zap that does five useful things per run costs five times a Zap that does one, and busy automations push you up the task tiers faster than the headline price suggests. The free plan's two step ceiling is the other wall: a trigger and one action is a demonstration, not a workflow. And if you reach your task limit without pay per task billing switched on, your Zaps pause until the next usage period, which is easy to miss if you are not watching for the warning emails.

If you leave

On both platforms your data lives in the apps at either end, so nothing is stranded when you go. The workflows are what stay behind. A Make scenario is a map and a Zap is a list; neither converts into the other, and moving means rebuilding each automation by hand and testing it again. The more branching and list handling you built, the longer the rebuild, on either side of this comparison.

Verdict

Choose Zapier if your automations are straight lines between popular apps, if you are new to automation, and if you would rather pay for finished work than learn a canvas. Its free plan is a fair trial of the shape, its checks cost nothing, and when a Zap fails it costs nothing either.

Choose Make if your automations branch, repeat over lists, or run often, and if the monthly bill matters to you. Core at $12 a month billed annually buys more automation than most solo businesses will use in a month, provided you treat scheduled checks as spending, because Make does.
