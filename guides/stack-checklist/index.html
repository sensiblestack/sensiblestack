The Small Business Stack Checklist

Five jobs every business of 1 to 100 people needs software for, in the order we would set them up. Start free, upgrade when the free version starts costing you customers, and skip everything that is not on this page until all five boxes are ticked.

How to use this checklist. Work down it in order. Each item names the job, the tool we would start with, the free way in, the signal that it is time to pay, and the trap to skip. Tick the box when the job is covered, not when the tool is bought: a free account you actually use beats a paid one gathering dust. You can print this page and it keeps its layout.

1. A website that runs itself

Our pick: WordPress on managed hosting, Kinsta or WP Engine, so backups, security and updates happen without you.

Start free or cheap: budget hosting or a simple site builder is fine at zero traffic.

Upgrade when: the site breaking, or you fixing it, starts costing more than the hosting upgrade.

Skip: page builder subscriptions stacked on page builder subscriptions. One theme, one builder, done.

2. An email list you own

Our pick: Kit. Social followers are rented; an email list is yours, and it is the cheapest follow up system a small business can own. See Kit vs Klaviyo for the full first hand comparison.

Start free: Kit's free plan covers broadcasts, forms and landing pages for a growing list.

Upgrade when: you need automations and sequences doing the follow up for you.

Skip: an ecommerce grade email platform if you do not sell products online. Depth you never use still bills monthly.

3. A CRM, so no enquiry gets lost

Our pick: start on the free HubSpot CRM, free for up to two users and genuinely usable. When follow up volume becomes real, bookings, quotes, reminders and SMS chasing, an all in one like HighLevel earns its $97 a month. The CRM field is compared in full on our HubSpot hub.

Start free: HubSpot free tools, today, before the next enquiry lands in your inbox and dies there.

Upgrade when: you can name the follow ups that slipped last month. That is the bill for not upgrading.

Skip: paying for CRM seats for people who do not exist yet.

4. A way to get found

Our pick: a free Semrush account for weekly checks, with a paid month in bursts when you have a real research job. The solo maths are in Is Semrush Worth It for a Solopreneur.

Start free: the free account covers keyword checks, a capped site audit and position tracking for a handful of terms.

Upgrade when: search is a real source of customers and you are publishing every week.

Skip: a full time SEO subscription before you publish regularly. The tool hands you a list of work. Publish first.

5. One place to run the work

Our pick: one project tool, matched to how your brain works, kept for at least a year. We run our own work in Notion; ClickUp and monday.com suit different heads, and all twelve of our reviews sit in the tools index.

Start free: every serious project tool has a free plan that covers a small team properly.

Upgrade when: the free plan's limits, seats, automations or history, block work that matters.

Skip: running two or three project tools at once. The best project tool is the one still open on your screen in month six.

6. Money basics, covered simply

The job: a separate business bank account, invoices that go out the day work is done, and receipts kept somewhere your accountant can reach. No software pick here on purpose: use the invoicing built into whatever accounting tool your accountant already likes, and spend the saved subscription money on the five jobs above.

Skip: a finance stack bought before the business has income to organise.

The order matters more than the tools

Website, list, CRM, search, work system. Each one makes the next worth more: traffic is wasted without a list to catch it, a list is wasted without follow up, and follow up is wasted if the work itself is chaos. Buy in order, start free, and let running out of room, not a sales page, tell you when to upgrade.
